# The Five Fires Manifesto

### A Managed Five-Party Democracy for America

*DeDomkop · 2026*

---

## The Core Mechanism

Five political parties hold formal recognition at any given time, determined by national vote share in each federal election. Recognition grants seats in Congress, debate access, and cabinet representation.

**Always five. Never more, never fewer.**

---

## How Parties Enter and Exit

After each federal election, national vote shares are tallied. The top five parties are recognized. If a new party outpolls a currently recognized party, the swap is immediate. The displaced party loses all institutional standing.

Unrecognized parties can still exist, organize, and run candidates. But they hold zero seats and zero formal role in governance until they earn a top-five position.

Votes cast for a party outside the top five elect nobody. This must be said plainly, because it is the sharpest edge in the design and the temptation is to soften it: those votes are not transferred to a second preference and they do not follow their voters anywhere. They fall out of the count, and the recognized five divide the chamber between them — so a wasted vote slightly enlarges every party its voter did not choose.

This edge is called the cliff, and it is doing deliberate work at both ends of the system. It is what makes recognition worth fighting for. It is also what stops fragmentation without any rule against fragmenting: a party of 8% that divides itself in half wins **nothing at all**, because both halves fall off the cliff.

The remedy the system offers is not a consolation transfer. It is organisation. Two parties of 8% and 7% who both expect to fall short can run on one ballot line and hold a fire comfortably. Four scattered tail parties totalling 24% can combine and take the largest fire in the chamber. The cliff does not tell small movements they are unwelcome; it tells them they are unorganised, and hands them the mechanism.

This creates a competitive ecosystem. Dozens of parties can compete, but only five govern. The system self-prunes without any authority deciding which parties are legitimate — every rule below reads vote counts and nothing else.

## The Two Moves

A party has exactly two moves, and both are made before the ballot, in public, with voters watching: **divide** or **merge**. Everything structural in this system is an attempt to price those two moves correctly and then get out of the way.

### The Cap, and the Cold Seats

No fire may hold more than 30% of the chamber. Seats above that line are left **cold** — vacant. They are not redistributed to rival parties.

A party winning 40% of the vote takes 120 of 400 seats and forfeits 73. Divided into two parties of 20%, the same voters return 210 seats and forfeit none. Refusing to divide is not penalised by a fine or a rule; it simply leaves seats unclaimed on the floor of the chamber, election after election, in plain view.

Dividing is therefore not a bonus. It is claiming seats you have already won.

Leaving the excess cold rather than handing it to rivals is the part that matters most, and it was not the first design. Redistribute those 73 seats and the runner-up finishes ahead of the party that won the most votes — a party with 20% of the vote seated above a party with 40%. No electorate would accept that, and no constitution should ask them to. Cold seats put the entire cost on the party that refuses to divide, and elevate nobody artificially. The largest fire remains the largest fire. It simply cannot govern alone.

### What the Cap Does Not Do

It does not force a party to divide sincerely. A party can split in name only, into two organisations sharing a leader, a donor list and a whip.

An earlier version of this proposal tried to prevent that by tracking lineage — ruling that parties descended from a common parent share one fire. That rule has been removed, and its removal is a genuine improvement rather than a concession. Enforcing it requires an authority to decide which parties are *really* the same, and that question is political, not administrative. Whoever answered it would become the most powerful office in the system and the first one worth capturing.

It is also unnecessary. A party that has divided in name only is free to merge back — and the cap takes the seats off it the moment it does. There is no test of sincerity because there is no referee to apply one, and none is needed.

### Merging, and Why the Cap Regulates It

Merging is the same move run backwards, and it is how the bottom of the system breathes. Two ejected parties can combine and clear the cliff. A scattered tail can combine and lead the council.

The cap limits it without a separate rule. A fire absorbing smaller parties gains seats right up to 30% of the chamber and **not one seat beyond**. Past the cap, absorbing more buys literally nothing while wasting what it takes. Consolidation stops dead at the same line that forces division.

Between them, the cap and the cliff bracket a fire at roughly a fifth to a third of the chamber. Merge upward off the cliff; divide downward off the cap. Nothing instructs a party to aim for a fifth of the vote. The two edges simply make anything else worse.

### A Note on the Arithmetic

Seats are allocated by Sainte-Laguë, an established proportional method already used in New Zealand and Norway.

An earlier version of this manifesto claimed the divisor itself rewards dividing — that a 40% party would gain seats by splitting under Sainte-Laguë. **That claim was false**, and it is worth correcting openly rather than quietly, because it was load-bearing. Proportional means proportional: under Sainte-Laguë *or* D'Hondt, a party that divides holds exactly the seats it held before, to the seat. No divisor supplies a splitting bonus, and any that appeared to would be non-proportional by definition and would reward fragmenting small parties into dust.

The apparent effect came from the recognition rule, not the arithmetic. Every incentive to divide in this system comes from the cap.

## Post-Count Rescue

Everything above happens before the ballot. There is one rule that does not. It is optional, currently switched on, and stated here at length rather than buried, because it is the one place the system breaks its own central principle.

After the count, parties the ballot left out may combine: either merging with each other to claim a fire, or joining a fire already tended by someone else. In the worked example this takes the wasted vote from 17% to as little as 1%.

The two forms are not equivalent, and the difference is the whole of the argument.

**Merging is never additive.** There are five fires. A rescue that wins one necessarily unseats a party that won one on the night. This is not a risk to be managed, it is an identity — in every simulation run, a rescue that claimed a fire displaced a seated party one hundred percent of the time. The honest description of the rule is therefore not "no vote is wasted" but *parties the ballot left out may combine to unseat the smallest party it seated.*

**Joining is additive.** Nobody is unseated, the fire count holds, and less vote is wasted than under merging. What the joiners do not get is a voice: they hold seats inside another party's fire and no chair in council.

So the rule puts a genuine choice to the tail — a voice of their own at a seated party's expense, or seats at nobody's. That is the same trade between seats and voices that the rest of the system runs on, offered to its smallest players.

Three limits keep it from becoming something else. A **participation floor** of 2% separates a rescue from a vacuum cleaner; without it, any quantity of dust can be assembled into a fire. **A fire already at the cap may not host**, because it gains no seats by taking more and could therefore outbid every other host at no cost to itself, purely to deny them — burning what it took. And **at most one seated party per rescue**, since two would be a post-count merger of fires, freeing a slot and admitting the sixth party by arrangement rather than by vote.

And the standing objection, which does not go away by being answered once: before the ballot, a voter can see a merger coming and judge it. After the ballot, they cannot. Every other rule in this system settles the chamber at the ballot; this one settles it in a room afterwards. Whether the recovered vote is worth that is a political judgement rather than a technical one, which is precisely why it is a switch and not a fixture.

## How Seats Are Allocated

### The House: 400 Proportional Seats

The House of Representatives holds 400 seats, distributed by proportional representation through multi-member districts. Each state's seats are allocated according to party vote share within that state. If a state holds ten seats and the vote splits 30-25-20-15-10, the seats follow that split — subject to the cap, which applies to a fire's holding in the chamber as a whole.

Multi-member districts eliminate gerrymandering entirely. There are no single-member district lines to manipulate. A party with 20% support in a state gets roughly 20% of that state's seats regardless of how its voters are geographically distributed.

An earlier version of this proposal added 35 bonus seats allocated in reverse order of party size, to compress the gap between large and small parties. Those are gone. The cap does the same work more honestly: rather than inventing seats to hand downward, it declines to seat a party beyond its share and leaves the difference visible and empty.

### The Senate

The Senate retains two seats per state. Senators are elected by ranked-choice voting. Voters rank candidates in order of preference. If no candidate wins an outright majority of first-choice votes, the lowest-ranked candidate is eliminated and their voters' second choices are redistributed. This continues until one candidate holds a majority.

Ranked-choice in the Senate produces senators who need appeal beyond a single party's base. A candidate despised by 60% of the state cannot win, even if the remaining 40% is intensely loyal.

## The President

### Election

The president is elected by two-round national popular vote. Any candidate — party-affiliated or independent — can enter the first round. All voters cast a single vote. The top two candidates proceed to a runoff two weeks later, in which the winner must secure a genuine majority.

This is open and direct. Party nominees compete alongside independents. No Electoral College. No ranked-choice complexity. Two rounds, clear majority, done.

### Powers

The president remains head of state, representing the nation domestically and internationally. The president retains the power to propose legislation, negotiate treaties, and nominate cabinet members and judges for Congressional confirmation.

The president does not have the power to deploy the military unilaterally. Any military deployment requires Congressional approval. The era of one person deciding to take a country to war is over.

### The Sliding Veto

The president retains veto power over legislation, but its strength is inversely proportional to the size of the Congressional majority that passed the bill.

A bill that passes with a bare majority can be vetoed outright. As the majority grows, the veto weakens — delaying the bill rather than killing it. Above a supermajority threshold, the veto becomes ineffective and the bill passes regardless of the president's objection.

The logic: if Congress barely agrees on something, the president's objection carries real weight. If four of five parties have voted in favor, one person's objection is not sufficient to override that level of democratic consensus.

This mechanic also incentivizes broader coalition-building within Congress. A party that wants veto-proof legislation cannot rely on a bare majority — it must build support across three or four parties, producing bills with genuine cross-partisan backing.

The sliding veto transforms the presidency from a gatekeeper into a pressure valve. It still provides a check on narrow majorities while respecting overwhelming consensus.

## The Council of Five

Each fire holds one seat on a council of five, chaired by the Firekeeper. One fire, one voice, regardless of size — a party holding 8% of the chamber speaks with the same weight there as a party holding 30%.

The councillor is elected by the seated members of their own fire, serves a fixed term, and **cannot be recalled by their party**. That clause is not procedural detail; it is the whole mechanism. A councillor who can be recalled is staff. One who cannot is a rival.

This is what turns a division into something real rather than cosmetic, and it does so without any rule requiring sincerity. Inside a party of 40%, a faction leader is a subordinate. Holding a fire makes that same person a constitutional officer with their own mandate and their own chair. Nobody hands such a thing back. Re-merging two fires means one of the two councillors loses their seat, so the division is defended by the person who gained from it — and defended against their own former leadership.

It works slowly. Two councillors from a freshly divided party will vote identically in the first cycle, and probably the second. What separates them is time and ambition: separate mandates, separate caucuses, separate ballot lines, and — because the council is the natural pool for the presidency and the Firekeeper's chair — a standing competition for the next promotion. Rivalry between peers who were recently colleagues is the most reliable force in politics. The council does not manufacture it. It simply builds the room where it happens.

You cannot legislate sincerity. You can create chairs.

## The Two Keys

Ordinary legislation needs a majority of the chamber and nothing more. The council plays no part in it.

Structural acts — overriding a veto, confirming judges, changing the rules themselves — need two keys turned at once: **three of the five fires in council, and two thirds of the chamber.**

The seat key is a supermajority rather than a majority, and the reason is specific. A single bloc that divides itself three ways can hold three of five fires *and* a bare majority of seats — in the worked example, 235 seats of 400. Three fires and a majority would let one party rewrite the constitution alone by the simple expedient of becoming three parties. Two thirds is out of reach: even dividing 52% of the national vote three ways yields only 65% of the chamber, because the wasted vote such a bloc is exploiting is shared out among its rivals as well.

One point of honesty about how the two keys currently interact. Because no fire may exceed 30% of the chamber, two fires can reach at most 60% — short of two thirds. Any coalition that clears the seat key therefore holds three fires automatically, which means that at these numbers **the council key never binds**. The cap has quietly taken over the job the council key was invented for.

That is not a flaw, but it should not be mistaken for a check either. The council key becomes live again the moment the cap rises above half the supermajority, and it remains in the constitution as insurance against exactly that. What it does unconditionally is decide *who deliberates*: five voices, one per fire, and the tiny fifth fire speaks at the same volume as the largest. That is only tolerable because the seat key stands behind it — small fires can block, but they cannot act.

## The Firekeeper

Congress as a whole elects one person through ranked-choice voting to serve as Firekeeper. The Firekeeper renounces party membership for the duration of their term.

The Firekeeper's role is strictly procedural:

- Chairs congressional procedure and floor debate
- Mediates disputes between parties during legislative negotiation
- Oversees election integrity and enforcement of the five-party rules
- Cannot introduce, sponsor, or co-sponsor legislation
- Cannot vote on legislation except to break a procedural tie

The Firekeeper is not a speaker in the traditional sense. They have no agenda-setting power and no partisan loyalty. Their authority is entirely structural — maintaining the process through which the five parties deliberate and legislate.

This creates an institutional role for a type of political figure that currently has no path to influence: the proceduralist whose ambition is to make governance function rather than to advance a partisan agenda. The role becomes an institution over time rather than a personality, which is the mark of a stable system.

## Referendums

Citizens can petition for a binding referendum on any issue. The petition threshold is set high enough to prevent frivolous use — a fixed percentage of eligible voters must sign before a referendum qualifies.

Once qualified, the referendum is put to a national vote. A simple majority of votes cast decides the outcome. The result is binding. Congress cannot override it.

There is one constraint: referendums cannot be used to remove or restrict the fundamental rights of any group. The system provides a direct check on representative government, not a mechanism for majorities to tyrannize minorities.

The referendum exists because no representative system perfectly reflects the will of the people it serves. Five parties are better than two, but they are still intermediaries. When the intermediaries fail — when Congress cannot act, will not act, or acts against the clear will of the population — citizens can bypass them entirely.

In a country that has never had a national referendum mechanism, this is a significant addition. It is also long overdue. Switzerland has operated binding referendums for over 170 years. The mechanism is proven. The absence of it in America is a structural gap, not a deliberate design choice — the Founders lacked the infrastructure for national popular votes. That limitation no longer exists.

## Coalition Dynamics

No party in this system can govern alone, and this is arithmetic rather than aspiration: the cap holds every fire to 30% of the chamber, so a majority always requires at least two fires and a structural act always requires at least three. This is not a flaw — it is the central design feature.

Different bills produce different coalitions. An infrastructure bill might unite an economic-populist party with an industry-focused party. A civil liberties bill might unite a progressive party with a libertarian-leaning party. A defense bill might unite a security-focused party with a centrist party. The coalitions are fluid and issue-specific.

This eliminates the permanent opposition dynamic of a two-party system, where one party's sole incentive is to block the other. In a five-party system, the party that opposes you on healthcare might be your essential partner on trade. Structural interdependence makes total obstruction irrational — if you refuse to negotiate on anything, the other four parties build coalitions without you and you get nothing for your voters.

Legislative productivity increases not because politicians become more cooperative by nature, but because the incentive structure makes cooperation the only viable path to delivering results.

## Why Five

Five is not arbitrary. It is the first number of parties at which stable multiparty dynamics emerge.

Two creates a binary with no mediating force. Three is unstable — two can always ally against the third, collapsing back into a two-against-one dynamic. Four polarizes into two blocs of two. At five, no coalition of two can dominate on its own. Shifting alliances form issue by issue. There is always a party outside any given coalition that can serve as a swing vote or mediating voice.

In a five-person group, three can outvote two. That is a 60% majority — a supermajority by most standards. And the two who lost are not permanently locked out. Next issue, different coalition, different three. The alliances stay fluid because the geometry of five makes permanent blocs hard to sustain.

The five parties do not need to be equal in size. One party might hold 30% of the chamber while another holds 8%, and in council those two speak with the same voice. The structure stays stable while the power balance stays fluid. Parties grow and shrink based on what voters need in each cycle. The architecture holds regardless of the distribution within it — the cap and the cliff simply make the extremes expensive, so the distribution tends to settle between roughly a fifth and a third without anyone being told to aim there.

## Historical Precedent

Penta-governance — the distribution of power across five co-equal bodies — is not a new idea. It has appeared independently across civilizations and centuries.

**The Haudenosaunee Confederacy** (c. 1142). Five nations — Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca — governed through the Great Law of Peace. Each sovereign, each distinct, bound by consensus and mutual accountability. The Peacemaker demonstrated the principle with arrows: a single arrow breaks easily, but five arrows bound together cannot be broken. The Confederacy lasted centuries and directly influenced the American Founders — Benjamin Franklin studied its structure and argued publicly that if five nations could sustain such a union, thirteen colonies could manage the same. The U.S. Senate formally recognized this influence in 1987. America borrowed the Haudenosaunee form but abandoned the philosophy. The Five Fires system proposes to reclaim what was left behind.

**The Christian Pentarchy** (c. 451–692). Five patriarchal sees — Rome, Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch, and Jerusalem — governed universal Christendom. Emperor Justinian called them "the five senses of the universe." Each patriarch was sovereign within their domain. The system held that it was impossible for all five to be in error simultaneously — a structural claim about the reliability of distributed judgment. It collapsed only when external conquest removed three of the five seats.

**The Concert of Europe** (1815–1914). After the Napoleonic wars, five great powers — Russia, Austria, Great Britain, Prussia, and France — maintained continental peace through structured multipolarity. No single power could dominate. The system held for nearly a century. It collapsed when German unification disrupted the five-way balance, leading to World War I.

**The Maratha Pentarchy** (c. 1772–1818). After the Battle of Panipat, the Maratha Empire fractured into a confederacy of five dynasties: the Peshwas of Pune, the Sindhias of Gwalior, the Holkars of Indore, the Bhonsles of Nagpur, and the Gaekwads of Baroda. Five was where the system naturally stabilized. It governed a third of the Indian subcontinent. But without structural binding rules, it degenerated into rivalry between the top two houses — Sindhia and Holkar — and the British picked the five apart one by one. The Maratha Pentarchy is the strongest argument both for and against penta-governance: the architecture works, but without engineered constraints it degrades into the same failure modes the Five Fires system is designed to prevent.

**Chinese Wu Xing** (ancient). Five elements — Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal, Water — in a generative and restraining cycle, applied to governance philosophy for millennia. The companion proposal to this manifesto, *The Five Elements Republic* (DeDomkop, 2026), applies this framework to the problem of Chinese unification through distributed functional governance.

The pattern is cross-civilizational: five co-equal forces produce stable systems that do not collapse from internal dynamics. Historical pentarchies failed only when external forces overwhelmed them or when the five-way balance lacked structural enforcement. The Five Fires system learns from both the successes and the failures.

## Related Work

The Five Fires system is a companion proposal to *The Five Elements Republic* (DeDomkop, 2026), which applies distributed governance principles to the problem of Chinese unification. Where the Five Fires system addresses American political stagnation through flexible multiparty competition, the Five Elements Republic addresses Chinese fragmentation through structured functional roles drawn from Chinese philosophical tradition.

The two systems share a core conviction: governance should be distributed across multiple balanced forces rather than concentrated in one or two. But they solve opposite problems. The Chinese system is centripetal — pulling separate entities into structured cooperation. The American system is centrifugal — breaking a calcified duopoly into dynamic competition. One prescribes defined roles. The other lets roles emerge from open competition.

Same philosophy. Opposite engineering. Different civilizations require different architectures.

## Summary

1. **Five parties, always five.** Top five by national vote share hold recognition. A sixth that outpolls a recognized party swaps in, the lowest drops out. Votes cast below the line elect nobody — the cliff is deliberate, and organising past it is the remedy.
2. **Proportional House, capped fires.** 400 seats by vote share per state. No fire may hold more than 30% of the chamber; seats above the cap are left cold rather than given to rivals. A party of 40% takes 120 seats and forfeits 73; divided in two it takes 210 and forfeits none.
3. **Ranked-choice Senate.** Two seats per state, elected by ranked-choice voting.
4. **President by two-round popular vote.** Open first round, top-two runoff. No unilateral military deployment. Sliding veto — strong against bare majorities, powerless against supermajorities.
5. **The Council of Five and the Firekeeper.** One voice per fire regardless of size, chaired by a Firekeeper elected by all of Congress who renounces party membership and cannot legislate. Councillors cannot be recalled by their parties.
6. **Two keys for structural acts.** Three of five fires in council, and two thirds of the chamber. Ordinary legislation needs a majority and nothing else.
7. **Binding referendums.** Citizen-petitioned, simple majority, binding. Cannot restrict fundamental rights.

Seven rules. A managed five-party democracy. A system designed not to depend on the goodness of the people inside it, but to function regardless.

And one rule that is not in the list: no rule above reads party identity, history or lineage. Every one of them reads vote counts. There is no registrar of who is really whom, no authority ruling on whether a division was sincere, and therefore nothing at the centre of this system worth capturing.
